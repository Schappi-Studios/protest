A student campaign · school name

Copilot isn’t allowed because it’s safe.
It’s allowed because it’s Microsoft.

Our school blocked every AI tool students actually use — and left exactly one standing. Not after a risk assessment. Not after asking a single teacher. The web filter shipped with a category called “Artificial Intelligence,” someone ticked it, and Copilot slipped through because it’s filed under Microsoft rather than under AI.

We are asking for that decision to be made properly, in writing, by a person. Starting with the tool that got taken away from us mid-coursework: Claude Code.

Sign the petition Read the three demands

01 — What actually happened

A filter category is not a policy

Web filters sold to schools ship with pre-built category lists. One of them is “AI & Chatbots.” Turning it on takes one click and blocks a few hundred domains at once. Nobody has to read anything, weigh anything, or put their name to anything.

That’s what happened here, and you can prove it from the results alone. Look at what survived:

Copilot runs on the same OpenAI models as the ChatGPT the filter blocks. If generative AI were genuinely judged too risky for students, the tool at the top of that table would be blocked too. It isn’t. So the line wasn’t drawn at risk. It was drawn at vendor.

Which means one of two things is true, and the school should tell us which: either there was a real assessment that cleared Copilot on grounds nobody has published — or there was no assessment at all.

we already know which one it is

02 — The ask

Three things. All of them reasonable.

  1. Unblock Claude Code for students doing coursework.

    Claude Code is a command-line developer tool — it runs in a terminal alongside git, npm and an editor. It is not a chatbot with a homework box. Blocking it is closer to blocking an IDE or Stack Overflow than to blocking a cheating site, and it lands hardest on exactly the students doing the most serious work.

  2. End the single-vendor exception.

    Either the major assistants are acceptable for student use or they aren’t. If Copilot is allowed, ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini need either the same access or a published, specific reason why one vendor clears a bar the others fail. “It came with the Microsoft licence” is not a safeguarding argument.

  3. Publish a written AI policy with a named owner and an appeal route.

    One page. What’s allowed, what isn’t, the reasoning, who signed it, and how a student or teacher requests an exception. Right now the policy is a checkbox in a vendor dashboard that no one will admit to ticking — and there is no way to challenge it, which is the part that should worry the adults more than it worries us.

03 — The objections

Everything they’ll say in the meeting

They say“Students will use it to cheat.”
The problem with thatCopilot writes essays. It’s allowed. The block doesn’t stop AI-written coursework — it just decides which company’s AI writes it. If academic integrity is the concern, the answer is an assessment policy, not a firewall rule.
They say“It’s a safeguarding requirement.”
The problem with thatThen name it. Safeguarding duties are written down and auditable; point to the clause that blocks Anthropic and clears Microsoft. Nobody is asking to remove content filtering or monitoring — we’re asking why one general-purpose assistant is exempt from a rule allegedly applied to all of them.
They say“Blocking it protects students from AI.”
The problem with thatIt doesn’t. Every student with a phone has unfiltered access from the car park. The block doesn’t remove AI from our education — it removes it from the one network where the school can log it, teach with it and set rules for it. It converts supervised use into unsupervised use and calls that a safeguard.
They say“Copilot does everything those tools do.”
The problem with thatNot for programming coursework. Claude Code works inside a real project — reading the repository, running the tests, making changes across files. That is how professional software is now written, and it’s what our CS students are supposed to be preparing for. A chat box in a browser tab is not a substitute for a tool that operates on the codebase.
They say“This is an IT decision, not an education decision.”
The problem with thatIt stopped being one the moment it changed what students are able to learn. IT owns how a policy is enforced. It doesn’t own whether a category of learning tool belongs in the curriculum — that belongs to teachers and the leadership team, and they should be told a decision was taken on their behalf.

04 — Scope

What we are not asking for

  • [ ]We are not asking for the content filter to be weakened, or for monitoring and safeguarding software to be removed. Leave all of it on.
  • [ ]We are not asking for permission to submit AI-written work. Set that rule at the assessment level, as strictly as you like, and enforce it there.
  • [ ]We are not asking anyone to be disciplined, named or blamed. We are asking for a decision to be reviewed by someone with the authority to make it.
  • [ ]We are not asking students to bypass the filter. Don’t — it hands them the excuse to make this about your conduct instead of their policy.

If the answer is a supervised compromise — opt-in access for CS and sixth-form students, a signed agreement, a teacher sponsor, revocable at any time — we’ll take it and say thank you. We are not trying to win an argument. We are trying to get a tool back.

06 — Do this today

A petition alone never moved an IT department

Signatures show the ask isn’t one annoyed student. What actually forces a review is a written request that lands in an inbox belonging to someone who has to answer it. Send this. It takes ninety seconds.

And three things that cost you nothing

  • 01Get one teacher to co-sign. A CS or DT teacher asking the same question changes it from a student complaint into a staff concern. This is the single highest-value thing on this page.
  • 02Put it on the student council agenda. It creates minutes. Minutes create a paper trail that someone eventually has to respond to.
  • 03Ask for the decision in writing. Not a reversal — just the reasoning, the owner, and the date. Requests that are hard to justify in writing tend to get quietly revisited.

07 — Escalation

You don’t know who blocked it. Find out.

Almost nobody does, which is the whole problem. Work up this ladder in order and don’t skip a rung — going over someone’s head first is the fastest way to make an ally into an obstacle.

  1. IT / network services

    Ask one question only: “Who owns the decision to block this category, and is there a documented exception process?” You’re not arguing yet — you’re finding the owner. Often they’ll tell you it was inherited from a filter default or a trust-wide setting, which is itself the answer you need.

  2. Head of Computer Science

    The person whose subject this damages most, and the one whose support carries the most weight in the next conversation. Bring the coursework angle, not the freedom angle.

  3. Senior leadership — the deputy head for curriculum or digital

    This is where the decision can actually be reversed. Lead with the three demands and the compromise offer. Bring the signature count and the teacher co-signature.

  4. Governors or trust board

    Slow, formal, and hard to ignore. Only worth it if leadership won’t engage. Most boards accept written questions from students, and “is there a written AI policy?” is a question a board is obliged to care about the answer to.
